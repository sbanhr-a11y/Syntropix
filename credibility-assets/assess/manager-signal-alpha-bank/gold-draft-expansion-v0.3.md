# GD-PJ-02 — Inherited underperformance after role change

Construct: People Judgment  
CX path: CX1 → CX2 → CX3

## Stage 1

You inherit a team member whose output has been below target for two months. The previous manager left little documentation.

A. Set a short improvement period immediately so expectations are clear while you learn more about the situation. **2**

B. Review available evidence, clarify role expectations with the employee, and understand barriers before choosing the intervention. **3**

C. Observe performance for several weeks without raising the issue so your judgment is independent of the previous manager. **1**

D. Ask close collaborators for examples of where the employee is struggling, then decide whether to discuss it formally. **2**

## Stage 2

The employee recently took responsibility for a new process and never received the planned training. Older responsibilities remain acceptable.

A. Restore the old responsibilities temporarily and delay performance judgment until formal training is complete. **2**

B. Keep the current role unchanged but give the employee additional time to learn independently. **1**

C. Reset expectations for the new process, provide targeted support, and define measurable outcomes over a short review period. **3**

D. Reduce the target for the new process permanently because the role transition was poorly managed. **1**

## Stage 3

After training, the new-process results improve, but another recurring task starts slipping.

A. Review total workload, priorities and handoffs with the employee before deciding whether the issue is capacity, prioritisation or capability. **3**

B. Extend the development period because the employee is still adapting to the broader role. **2**

C. Reassign the slipping task to another team member and keep the employee focused on the newly learned process. **1**

D. Start formal performance action because a second area of weakness now indicates a broader performance problem. **2**

# GD-PJ-03 — Confidential concern about another manager

Construct: People Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX4

## Stage 1

An employee from another team asks for a private conversation. They describe repeated humiliating comments by their manager and ask you not to tell anyone because they fear retaliation.

A. Explain that you will handle the information carefully but cannot promise absolute confidentiality before knowing whether escalation is required. **3**

B. Agree to keep the discussion confidential unless you personally conclude there is an immediate safety risk. **2**

C. Encourage the employee to speak directly with their manager first, offering to help them prepare for the conversation. **1**

D. Ask whether they are comfortable with you informally checking with other colleagues before deciding what to do. **1**

## Stage 2

The employee provides specific examples and says two colleagues witnessed them.

A. Explain the appropriate escalation route, limit disclosure to people who need to know, and avoid conducting an informal investigation outside your role. **3**

B. Speak confidentially to the witnesses first so you can determine whether formal escalation is justified. **2**

C. Give the employee a choice between formal escalation and keeping the matter fully confidential. **1**

D. Ask the manager for their perspective without identifying the employee who raised the concern. **1**

## Stage 3

The manager owns a business-critical launch, and senior leadership is concerned that any formal process could disrupt delivery.

A. Continue the appropriate concern-handling process and separately establish business-continuity measures for the launch. **3**

B. Accelerate the initial fact review so leadership can decide whether the concern is serious enough to justify disruption. **2**

C. Delay formal action until the most critical launch milestone passes, while monitoring for further concerns. **1**

D. Move launch responsibilities temporarily before the concern has been reviewed, to protect both employees and delivery. **2**

# GD-EP-02 — Product launch during an emerging incident

Construct: Execution & Prioritisation  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX4

## Stage 1

A major launch is scheduled tomorrow. A production issue starts affecting a small but growing group of existing customers. The same specialists are needed for both.

A. Keep launch staffing unchanged while a smaller incident team confirms whether the customer impact is still growing. **2**

B. Pause the launch immediately so all specialists can focus on protecting existing customers. **1**

C. Assess incident trajectory and launch consequences, then reallocate critical expertise according to risk rather than sunk launch effort. **3**

D. Let the technical specialists choose which work needs them most, then adjust the business plan around their decision. **2**

## Stage 2

The incident is not yet severe, but evidence suggests it could spread quickly.

A. Create an explicit go/no-go checkpoint, move enough expertise to containment, and preserve only launch work that does not weaken incident response. **3**

B. Continue the launch unless impact crosses the formal severity threshold, but prepare specialists to switch if needed. **2**

C. Delay the launch now because the possible downside of escalation is larger than the cost of one postponement. **2**

D. Keep both workstreams fully staffed by temporarily extending hours for the specialist team. **1**

## Stage 3

A senior sponsor says delaying the launch would break a public commitment.

A. Keep the risk-based go/no-go criteria and escalate the reputational trade-off rather than lowering the operational threshold. **3**

B. Proceed with a limited launch so the public commitment is technically met while exposure remains contained. **2**

C. Delay the launch and ask communications to manage the commitment externally. **2**

D. Proceed as planned if the technical owner is willing to formally accept the incident risk. **1**

# GD-EP-03 — Delegation under executive deadline pressure

Construct: Execution & Prioritisation  
CX path: CX1 → CX2 → CX3

A. Delegate the analysis with clear output standards/checkpoints while retaining responsibility for final executive synthesis. **3**

C. Give the employee the full board pack because the stretch assignment will accelerate development. **1**

D. Split the analysis between yourself and the employee so no single person carries too much deadline risk. **2**

## Stage 2

At the checkpoint, the analysis is mostly strong but one conclusion is not adequately supported.

A. Correct the conclusion yourself so the employee can continue with the remaining work. **2**

B. Explain the evidence gap and ask the employee to revise it while preserving their ownership of the delegated analysis. **3**

C. Remove the unsupported conclusion and leave that section out of the board pack. **2**

D. Keep the conclusion but label it as preliminary so the board can decide how much weight to give it. **1**

## Stage 3

A last-minute executive request adds a new analysis that cannot be completed reliably before the meeting.

A. Provide a clearly labelled preliminary estimate based on available data and commit to a verified follow-up. **2**

B. State what can be supported now, identify what remains unknown, and avoid presenting an unverified conclusion as complete. **3**

C. Ask for the board discussion to omit the topic because the evidence cannot be completed in time. **2**

D. Give the employee authority to choose the best estimate because they now know the analysis most deeply. **1**

# GD-DE-02 — Strong pilot result with a weak comparison

Construct: Decision & Evidence Quality  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX4

## Stage 1

A new process was piloted in one team. Productivity rose 12%. The sponsor wants company-wide rollout.

A. Expand to several additional teams but retain the ability to stop if performance does not replicate. **2**

B. Examine baseline differences and concurrent changes before attributing the improvement to the process. **3**

C. Roll out broadly because a 12% improvement creates too much opportunity cost to wait. **1**

D. Keep the process confined to the pilot team until a formal control comparison can be designed. **2**

## Stage 2

The pilot team also received additional staffing during the same period.

A. Treat attribution as uncertain and design the next rollout so staffing and process effects can be distinguished more clearly. **3**

B. Adjust the 12% improvement downward using an estimate of how much staffing probably contributed. **1**

C. Stop the rollout because the pilot can no longer establish that the process caused improvement. **2**

D. Continue a limited rollout, but use teams with similar staffing levels to the pilot group. **2**

## Stage 3

The sponsor says waiting could lose a quarter of potential benefit.

A. Run a reversible staged expansion with predefined success/failure measures and explicit attribution limitations. **3**

B. Roll out to half the organisation now, then compare those teams with the remainder after one quarter. **2**

C. Continue only with teams volunteering to adopt the process so implementation quality remains high. **1**

D. Delay until a more rigorous experiment can isolate the process effect with confidence. **2**

# GD-DE-03 — Vendor business case under sponsor pressure

Construct: Decision & Evidence Quality  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX4

## Stage 1

A vendor presents a polished analysis showing its solution will save 20% annually. Key assumptions are not fully disclosed.

A. Request the assumptions and independently test the inputs that materially drive the decision. **3**

B. Apply a conservative discount to the savings estimate and proceed only if the business case still works. **2**

C. Compare the vendor's estimate with results reported by reference customers before deciding whether deeper analysis is necessary. **2**

D. Reject the case until the vendor provides a fully auditable model. **1**

## Stage 2

The vendor discloses the model. A major benefit assumes unusually high user adoption.

A. Build adoption scenarios and assess whether the investment remains attractive under more realistic ranges. **3**

B. Ask the vendor to contractually guarantee the benefit if adoption assumptions prove too optimistic. **1**

C. Use the vendor's assumption but require a stronger internal change-management programme. **2**

D. Replace the vendor assumption with adoption achieved in your most comparable prior rollout. **2**

## Stage 3

Your executive sponsor strongly prefers this vendor and wants approval before quarter-end.

A. Present a decision range with key assumptions and mitigation, allowing approval against explicit uncertainty rather than one headline estimate. **3**

B. Approve conditionally, with implementation milestones that allow the organisation to exit if adoption is weak. **2**

C. Delay approval until the sponsor agrees to an independent review of the business case. **1**

D. Approve if the discounted base-case return remains above the organisation's investment threshold. **2**

D. Offer a workaround first, then revisit whether the full feature is still necessary. **2**

## Stage 2

Product says the feature is possible only by delaying another committed customer item.

A. Surface the cross-customer trade-off to the accountable prioritisation owner before making either commitment. **3**

B. Prioritise the larger customer's request because revenue exposure is greater. **1**

C. Offer the requesting customer a reduced-scope feature that engineering believes can fit without delaying the other commitment. **2**

D. Ask both account teams to quantify customer impact before Product makes the decision. **2**

## Stage 3

Sales leadership wants a date communicated before prioritisation is resolved.

A. Communicate the earliest plausible date as provisional, with clear dependency on final prioritisation. **2**

B. Give the customer a firm internal decision date/process rather than an unapproved delivery promise. **3**

C. Ask Sales leadership to own the provisional commitment if they want it communicated early. **1**

D. Wait to communicate until Product finalises prioritisation, to avoid creating another expectation. **2**

# GD-SJ-03 — Cross-functional ownership gap

Construct: Stakeholder Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX3

## Stage 1

A recurring customer issue spans your team and another function. Each side believes the other owns the root cause.

A. Bring relevant owners together around the customer impact, map dependencies/accountabilities, and establish one resolution path. **3**

B. Fix what your team can control immediately, then document the remaining dependency for the other function. **2**

C. Escalate both functions to the shared senior leader so ownership can be decided objectively. **2**

D. Agree a temporary shared-ownership model until the technical root cause can be confirmed. **2**

## Stage 2

The other manager agrees to actions but repeatedly misses them.

A. Make commitments, dates and customer impact visible; address the misses directly and escalate only if the dependency remains unresolved. **3**

B. Reassign as much of their work as possible into your team so the customer no longer depends on the missed commitment. **1**

C. Escalate now because repeated misses show the peer-level process has failed. **2**

D. Agree smaller milestones with the other manager so progress can be monitored more closely. **2**

## Stage 3

Senior leadership asks which team caused the problem.

A. Explain the shared failure mode, distinguish accountabilities factually, and focus on the system/control needed to prevent recurrence. **3**

B. State which team's missed actions contributed most, while also explaining the cross-functional dependencies. **2**

C. Avoid attributing responsibility because doing so could damage the working relationship needed for the fix. **1**

D. Present both teams as jointly responsible because the issue crossed functional boundaries. **1**

# GD-LA-02 — Feedback challenges your delegation style

Construct: Learning & Adaptive Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX3

## Stage 1

Your engagement survey says employees want more autonomy. You believe you already delegate extensively.

A. Ask for specific examples of where people experience unnecessary control and compare those examples with your intended delegation. **3**

B. Increase delegation immediately in a few low-risk areas while gathering more detail on the survey result. **2**

C. Explain to the team where they already have decision rights, then ask whether the issue is clarity rather than autonomy. **2**

D. Wait for the next survey cycle so you can tell whether the result is stable before changing your management approach. **1**

## Stage 2

Examples show you frequently revise employees' work before stakeholders see it.

A. Stop reviewing most work and intervene only when someone specifically asks for help. **1**

B. Define which outputs are genuinely high-risk and test lighter review/checkpoints for normal work. **3**

C. Keep final review but tell employees explicitly which changes are optional versus required. **2**

D. Ask employees to submit work earlier so they have time to incorporate your review without feeling controlled. **1**

## Stage 3

One lightly reviewed output contains a visible mistake.

A. Diagnose whether the mistake reflects capability, a missing control or the new delegation model, then adjust proportionately. **3**

B. Reinstate review for that employee until their work has been error-free for a defined period. **2**

C. Return to reviewing stakeholder-facing work but keep lighter controls for internal outputs. **2**

D. Continue the experiment unchanged because one error is insufficient evidence to revise the model. **1**

# GD-LA-03 — New operating model changes decision rights

Construct: Learning & Adaptive Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX3

## Stage 1

A new operating model transfers some of your team's decision authority to a central function. Your team believes work will slow.

A. Clarify the intended boundaries, identify likely friction points, and test the model using real cases before judging whether it works. **3**

B. Implement the model as designed for one quarter, collect evidence, then propose changes if delays materialise. **2**

C. Ask leadership to clarify the benefits before requiring your team to change a process that currently works. **1**

D. Preserve local decisions for urgent work while using the central function for normal cases. **1**

## Stage 2

Early cases are slower, but central review catches risks your team previously missed.

A. Accept the slower process because the additional risk control demonstrates why centralisation was needed. **2**

B. Use the evidence to propose risk-based thresholds that preserve central review for higher-risk work and faster handling for lower-risk cases. **3**

C. Ask the central function to commit to tighter service levels before discussing changes to decision rights. **2**

D. Reintroduce local approval for time-sensitive cases and document the exceptions. **1**

## Stage 3

The central leader initially rejects your threshold proposal.

A. Bring specific case evidence, acknowledge the risk benefit, and refine the proposal around shared outcomes. **3**

B. Escalate the issue because the central leader is not responding to operational evidence. **1**

C. Continue the current model but collect a larger sample of delays/risks before reopening the proposal. **2**

D. Negotiate a small pilot of the thresholds in one work category rather than asking for a broader policy change. **2**

# GD-AI-03 — Forecast model conflicts with field evidence

Construct: AI-era Management Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX4

## Stage 1

An approved forecasting tool recommends reducing inventory. An experienced employee disagrees because of recent customer conversations.

A. Ask the employee to quantify the field evidence, but follow the model unless that evidence reaches a predefined threshold. **2**

B. Examine the model inputs/assumptions alongside the employee's evidence before committing to either recommendation. **3**

C. Follow the employee this time because the model may lag real market conditions. **1**

D. Take an intermediate inventory position to reduce the risk of either source being wrong. **2**

## Stage 2

You discover the model excludes a recent competitor exit that materially changes demand conditions.

A. Override the current recommendation, document the missing variable, and request an updated analysis before treating the model as decision-ready. **3**

B. Adjust the model recommendation manually using the employee's estimate of the competitor effect. **2**

C. Ignore the model for the next planning cycle because the omitted event makes current outputs unreliable. **2**

D. Keep the model recommendation but increase inventory monitoring so any error can be corrected quickly. **1**

## Stage 3

The updated model now partially supports the employee's view but remains highly uncertain.

A. Make a staged/reversible inventory decision, monitor leading indicators, and keep both model and field evidence visible. **3**

B. Follow the updated model because its known limitation has now been corrected. **2**

C. Follow the employee because their original judgment identified the model's blind spot. **1**

D. Hold the current inventory level until the model's confidence improves enough to support a clearer choice. **2**
