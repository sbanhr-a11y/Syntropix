# GD-SJ-02 — Customer asks for an uncommitted feature

Construct: Stakeholder Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX3

## Stage 1

A major customer asks for a feature next month. Sales has suggested it is likely, but Product has not committed.

A. Clarify the customer's underlying need and give a provisional date while you confirm feasibility internally.

B. Avoid confirming a delivery date, clarify the need, and align internally on feasible options before committing.

C. Tell the customer Product has not approved the request and ask Sales to correct the expectation.

D. Offer a workaround first, then revisit whether the full feature is still necessary.

## Stage 2

Product says the feature is possible only by delaying another committed customer item.

A. Surface the cross-customer trade-off to the accountable prioritisation owner before making either commitment.

B. Prioritise the larger customer's request because revenue exposure is greater.

C. Offer the requesting customer a reduced-scope feature that engineering believes can fit without delaying the other commitment.

D. Ask both account teams to quantify customer impact before Product makes the decision.

## Stage 3

Sales leadership wants a date communicated before prioritisation is resolved.

A. Communicate the earliest plausible date as provisional, with clear dependency on final prioritisation.

B. Give the customer a firm internal decision date/process rather than an unapproved delivery promise.

C. Ask Sales leadership to own the provisional commitment if they want it communicated early.

D. Wait to communicate until Product finalises prioritisation, to avoid creating another expectation.

# GD-SJ-03 — Cross-functional ownership gap

Construct: Stakeholder Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX3

## Stage 1

A recurring customer issue spans your team and another function. Each side believes the other owns the root cause.

A. Bring relevant owners together around the customer impact, map dependencies/accountabilities, and establish one resolution path.

B. Fix what your team can control immediately, then document the remaining dependency for the other function.

C. Escalate both functions to the shared senior leader so ownership can be decided objectively.

D. Agree a temporary shared-ownership model until the technical root cause can be confirmed.

## Stage 2

The other manager agrees to actions but repeatedly misses them.

A. Make commitments, dates and customer impact visible; address the misses directly and escalate only if the dependency remains unresolved.

B. Reassign as much of their work as possible into your team so the customer no longer depends on the missed commitment.

C. Escalate now because repeated misses show the peer-level process has failed.

D. Agree smaller milestones with the other manager so progress can be monitored more closely.

## Stage 3

Senior leadership asks which team caused the problem.

A. Explain the shared failure mode, distinguish accountabilities factually, and focus on the system/control needed to prevent recurrence.

B. State which team's missed actions contributed most, while also explaining the cross-functional dependencies.

C. Avoid attributing responsibility because doing so could damage the working relationship needed for the fix.

D. Present both teams as jointly responsible because the issue crossed functional boundaries.

# GD-LA-02 — Feedback challenges your delegation style

Construct: Learning & Adaptive Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX3

## Stage 1

Your engagement survey says employees want more autonomy. You believe you already delegate extensively.

A. Ask for specific examples of where people experience unnecessary control and compare those examples with your intended delegation.

B. Increase delegation immediately in a few low-risk areas while gathering more detail on the survey result.

C. Explain to the team where they already have decision rights, then ask whether the issue is clarity rather than autonomy.

D. Wait for the next survey cycle so you can tell whether the result is stable before changing your management approach.

## Stage 2

Examples show you frequently revise employees' work before stakeholders see it.

A. Stop reviewing most work and intervene only when someone specifically asks for help.

B. Define which outputs are genuinely high-risk and test lighter review/checkpoints for normal work.

C. Keep final review but tell employees explicitly which changes are optional versus required.

D. Ask employees to submit work earlier so they have time to incorporate your review without feeling controlled.

## Stage 3

One lightly reviewed output contains a visible mistake.

A. Diagnose whether the mistake reflects capability, a missing control or the new delegation model, then adjust proportionately.

B. Reinstate review for that employee until their work has been error-free for a defined period.

C. Return to reviewing stakeholder-facing work but keep lighter controls for internal outputs.

D. Continue the experiment unchanged because one error is insufficient evidence to revise the model.

# GD-LA-03 — New operating model changes decision rights

Construct: Learning & Adaptive Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX3

## Stage 1

A new operating model transfers some of your team's decision authority to a central function. Your team believes work will slow.

A. Clarify the intended boundaries, identify likely friction points, and test the model using real cases before judging whether it works.

B. Implement the model as designed for one quarter, collect evidence, then propose changes if delays materialise.

C. Ask leadership to clarify the benefits before requiring your team to change a process that currently works.

D. Preserve local decisions for urgent work while using the central function for normal cases.

## Stage 2

Early cases are slower, but central review catches risks your team previously missed.

A. Accept the slower process because the additional risk control demonstrates why centralisation was needed.

B. Use the evidence to propose risk-based thresholds that preserve central review for higher-risk work and faster handling for lower-risk cases.

C. Ask the central function to commit to tighter service levels before discussing changes to decision rights.

D. Reintroduce local approval for time-sensitive cases and document the exceptions.

## Stage 3

The central leader initially rejects your threshold proposal.

A. Bring specific case evidence, acknowledge the risk benefit, and refine the proposal around shared outcomes.

B. Escalate the issue because the central leader is not responding to operational evidence.

C. Continue the current model but collect a larger sample of delays/risks before reopening the proposal.

D. Negotiate a small pilot of the thresholds in one work category rather than asking for a broader policy change.

# GD-AI-01 — Confidential client information and AI

Construct: AI-era Management Judgment  
CX path: CX1 → CX2 → CX3

## Stage 1

A team member wants to paste a confidential client document into a public generative-AI tool to create a summary quickly.

A. Allow it after removing obvious identifiers such as client name and contact details.

B. Stop the upload and use only an approved environment/process that permits the information and task.

C. Ask the employee to extract only the sections relevant to the summary, then use the public tool on those excerpts.

D. Complete this summary manually but allow the team to use public AI for less sensitive client material.

## Stage 2

An approved enterprise AI environment is available, but outputs may still contain errors.

A. Use the tool for the first draft and require verification against source material for claims that matter to the decision.

B. Use the AI summary only as an internal aid, while the human author writes the final summary separately.

C. Let the AI produce the final summary if the enterprise tool retains citations to the source document.

D. Restrict AI use to extracting facts and prohibit it from synthesising conclusions.

## Stage 3

The summary will inform a material commercial decision later the same day.

A. Increase verification for claims material to the decision and make source/uncertainty boundaries visible to the decision-maker.

B. Ask a second employee to review the AI summary rather than requiring the original user to verify every important claim.

C. Use the summary with a prominent AI-generated disclaimer because the deadline limits deeper review.

D. Delay the commercial decision until the entire summary can be independently verified line by line.

# GD-AI-03 — Forecast model conflicts with field evidence

Construct: AI-era Management Judgment  
CX path: CX2 → CX3 → CX4

## Stage 1

An approved forecasting tool recommends reducing inventory. An experienced employee disagrees because of recent customer conversations.

A. Ask the employee to quantify the field evidence, but follow the model unless that evidence reaches a predefined threshold.

B. Examine the model inputs/assumptions alongside the employee's evidence before committing to either recommendation.

C. Follow the employee this time because the model may lag real market conditions.

D. Take an intermediate inventory position to reduce the risk of either source being wrong.

## Stage 2

You discover the model excludes a recent competitor exit that materially changes demand conditions.

A. Override the current recommendation, document the missing variable, and request an updated analysis before treating the model as decision-ready.

B. Adjust the model recommendation manually using the employee's estimate of the competitor effect.

C. Ignore the model for the next planning cycle because the omitted event makes current outputs unreliable.

D. Keep the model recommendation but increase inventory monitoring so any error can be corrected quickly.

## Stage 3

The updated model now partially supports the employee's view but remains highly uncertain.

A. Make a staged/reversible inventory decision, monitor leading indicators, and keep both model and field evidence visible.

B. Follow the updated model because its known limitation has now been corrected.

C. Follow the employee because their original judgment identified the model's blind spot.

D. Hold the current inventory level until the model's confidence improves enough to support a clearer choice.
